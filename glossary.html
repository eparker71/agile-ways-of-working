Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms used across the toolkit. Orange notes give where a term comes from, or where frameworks and tools use it differently.

ABCDEFGIJKLMNOPRSTVW

A

Acceptance criteria

The conditions a backlog item must meet to be accepted. Often written as concrete examples in Given / When / Then form, so they double as tests.

See: Example Mapping · Backlog Item Anatomy

Affinity estimation

Sizing a whole backlog quickly by silently placing items in order from smallest to largest, then drawing size boundaries.

See: Estimation

Agile Release Train (ART)

A long-lived team of agile teams, typically 50 to 125 people, that plans, delivers and improves together on a shared cadence toward common goals.

SAFe term. Other names: team of teams; Scrum of Scrums (Scrum@Scale).

See: Cross-Team Planning · Cross-Team Sync · Dependency Map

AI autonomy levels

How much an AI tool or agent may do on its own: it assists while a person decides, it acts and a person approves the result, or it acts alone within set limits and people check afterwards.

See: AI in Delivery · AI Working Agreement

Architecture decision record (ADR)

A short document recording one important technical decision: its context, the options considered, the decision and its consequences.

Format described by Michael Nygard.

See: Architecture Decision Record

B

Backlog

An ordered list of the work a team or product might do. The top is detailed and ready; the bottom is rough ideas. The order is the plan.

Scrum calls it the Product Backlog, owned by the Product Owner.

See: Prioritisation Board · Backlog Item Anatomy

Benefits realisation

Checking, after a project ends, that the value it was meant to create really arrives, with a named owner and dates to measure each benefit.

Common in project and programme management.

See: Project Close-out · Value Metrics

Blameless review

A review of an incident that looks at how the system made it possible, not at who to blame, so people share what really happened.

Common in software reliability engineering.

See: Blameless Incident Review · Root Cause Analysis

Blocker

Something stopping a single work item from moving forward right now, such as waiting for a review or an environment. Compare impediment.

See: Blocker Clustering · Buffering & Swarming

Blue-green deployment

Running two copies of production: the new version goes to the idle copy, traffic switches to it, and switching back undoes the release.

See: Release Readiness & Strategy

Brooks's law

Adding people to a late software project makes it later, because new people need time to get up to speed and every extra person adds communication overhead.

From Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975).

See: Right-Size Your Structure

Burn-up chart

A chart showing work completed rising toward a total-scope line over time. Unlike a burn-down, it makes scope changes visible.

See: Release Forecast & Burn-up

C

Canary release

Releasing a change to a small share of users first, watching for problems, then widening it.

See: Release Readiness & Strategy

Capacity

How much work a team can realistically take on in a period, after holidays, other commitments and a buffer for unplanned work.

See: Sprint Planning · Buffering & Swarming

Class of service

A policy for how a type of work is treated, such as Expedite, Fixed date, Standard or Intangible.

From the Kanban Method.

See: Kanban Board Design

Cognitive load

How much a team has to hold in its head to do its work: domains, systems and tools. Too much slows everything down.

From Team Topologies (Skelton & Pais).

See: Team Topology Design

Continuous delivery (CD)

Keeping the software ready to release at any time: every change that passes the pipeline could go to production at the push of a button.

Described by Jez Humble and David Farley.

See: Delivery Pipeline Canvas · Continuous Delivery Health Check · Release Readiness & Strategy

Continuous deployment

Going one step further than continuous delivery: every change that passes the pipeline goes to production automatically, with no manual release step.

See: Delivery Pipeline Canvas · Release Readiness & Strategy

Continuous integration (CI)

Everyone merges small changes into the main branch often, at least daily, and every merge is built and tested automatically, so problems show up within minutes.

Practice popularised by Extreme Programming (XP).

See: Delivery Pipeline Canvas · Continuous Delivery Health Check

Cost of delay

The value lost for each week an item isn't delivered. Dividing it by size (CD3, or WSJF in SAFe) gives a priority order.

Popularised by Don Reinertsen.

See: Prioritisation Board · Portfolio Kanban & Investment Mix

Customer journey map

A picture of the stages a user goes through to reach a goal, with what they do, think and feel, pain points and opportunities.

See: Persona & Journey Map

Cycle time

How long an item takes from starting work to done. Look at percentiles, not averages. Compare lead time.

See: Ageing WIP & SLE · Kanban Board Design

Cynefin®

A sense-making framework that sorts situations into clear, complicated, complex and chaotic domains (with confusion in the centre), each calling for a different way to act.

From Dave Snowden. Cynefin® is a registered trademark of Cognitive Edge Pte Ltd.

See: Cynefin® Sense-Making

D

Daily Scrum

The team's daily 15-minute event to inspect progress toward the sprint goal and plan the next day. Often called a stand-up.

A Scrum event.

See: Daily Sync · Events & Cadences

Definition of Done

The shared quality bar every item must meet before it counts as done, such as tested, reviewed and releasable.

Part of Scrum, where it's the commitment for the Increment.

See: Team Charter · Kanban Board Design

Definition of Ready

A team's checklist for when an item is clear and small enough to start. Best used as a guide, not a gate.

Not part of the Scrum Guide; a common team practice.

See: Team Charter · Example Mapping

Delegation poker

A game for agreeing decision rights: for each decision area, everyone shows a card from 1 (Tell) to 7 (Delegate) at once, then discusses the differences.

From Management 3.0 (Jurgen Appelo).

See: Delegation Board

Delivery pipeline

The automated path a change takes from commit to production: build, test, checks, deploy and release. Also called a CI/CD pipeline or deployment pipeline.

See: Delivery Pipeline Canvas

Dependency

Something one team needs from another person, team or supplier before it can finish its work.

See: Dependency Map · RAID Log

Design sprint

A five-day process for answering a big product question: map, sketch, decide, prototype and test with five customers, with a named Decider.

From Jake Knapp at Google Ventures, described in Sprint (2016).

See: Design Sprint

DevOps

A way of working where development and operations share responsibility for building, releasing and running software, supported by automation and fast feedback.

See: Delivery Pipeline Canvas · Continuous Delivery Health Check · Blameless Incident Review

Domain event

Something meaningful that happened in the business, written in the past tense, such as “Order Placed”.

Used in Event Storming and domain-driven design.

See: Event Storming

DORA metrics

Four measures of delivery performance from the DORA research programme: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to restore service.

DORA: DevOps Research and Assessment.

See: Delivery Metrics & Tech Debt · Continuous Delivery Health Check

Dot voting

A quick way to prioritise: everyone places a few dots on the options at the same time, and the most dots win.

See: Facilitation Techniques · Lean Coffee

E

Enabler

Work that doesn't deliver user value directly but makes future value possible, such as paying down technical debt or building infrastructure.

SAFe uses this term formally; other teams say technical work or tech debt.

See: Delivery Metrics & Tech Debt · Backlog Item Anatomy

Epic

A big bet that should deliver a business outcome, too large for one iteration and broken into smaller items.

Meaning varies: in SAFe an epic sits above features; in Jira an epic sits directly above stories; Scrum doesn't define epics at all.

See: Epic Breakdown Canvas · Backlog Item Anatomy

ESVP

A quick, anonymous check-in at the start of a retrospective: each person says whether they feel like an Explorer, Shopper, Vacationer or Prisoner, which shows how engaged and safe the group feels.

See: Retrospective Kit

Event Storming

A workshop where business experts and engineers map a domain as a timeline of domain events on orange stickies, then add commands, actors, policies, systems and hotspots.

From Alberto Brandolini.

See: Event Storming

Experiment

A small, time-boxed test of a hypothesis, with a measure agreed in advance, so the result tells you whether to persevere, pivot or stop.

See: 1% Experiment Canvas · Opportunity Solution Tree

Exploratory testing

People testing the software freely, learning as they go, to find problems that scripted tests don't look for.

See: Test Strategy

F

Feature

A capability users can see and use that delivers part of an epic's value.

In SAFe, a feature fits within one planning increment; elsewhere the term is used more loosely.

See: Epic Breakdown Canvas · Backlog Item Anatomy

Feature flag

A switch in the code that turns a feature on or off without deploying again, so code can be deployed switched off and released later, or to some users only. Also called a feature toggle.

See: Release Readiness & Strategy · Continuous Delivery Health Check

Fist of five

A quick check of support: everyone shows zero to five fingers at once, and the lowest votes explain what's missing.

See: Facilitation Techniques · Sprint Planning

Flow efficiency

The share of an item's elapsed time spent actively working on it, rather than waiting. Often surprisingly low.

See: Kanban Board Design

Four big risks

The product risks to check before building: value (will they want it?), usability (can they use it?), feasibility (can we build it?) and viability (does it work for the business?).

From Marty Cagan, Inspired.

See: Product Bet Canvas

G

Go / no-go

A short decision, against a checklist agreed in advance, on whether a release goes ahead.

See: Release Readiness & Strategy

I

Impact mapping

A tree from a business goal to the actors who affect it, the behaviour changes needed, and the deliverables that might cause them.

From Gojko Adzic.

See: Impact Map

Impediment

Anything limiting how well the team works that it can't easily fix alone, such as slow approvals or missing tools. Compare blocker.

A Scrum term; removing impediments is part of the Scrum Master's role.

See: Impediments Board

Increment

A usable, done piece of product produced by the team, which adds to everything delivered before.

A Scrum term.

See: Sprint Review

Inspect & Adapt

A multi-team event at the end of a planning period: a demo of the integrated work, a review of measures and a problem-solving workshop.

A SAFe event; similar to LeSS's Overall Retrospective.

See: Cross-Team Retro · Events & Cadences

Iteration

A short, fixed timebox, usually one to four weeks, in which a team plans, builds and reviews work.

Scrum calls it a Sprint. Kanban doesn't use iterations; work flows continuously.

See: Sprint Planning · Sprint Review

J

Jobs to be done

A way of describing what users want in terms of progress: “When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].”

Associated with Clayton Christensen and Tony Ulwick.

See: Persona & Journey Map · Opportunity Solution Tree

K

Kano model

A way to classify features by how they affect satisfaction: basics users simply expect, performance features where more is better, and delighters nobody asked for.

From Professor Noriaki Kano.

See: Product Vision Board · Opportunity Solution Tree

Kudo cards

Short written thank-yous for something specific a colleague did, given peer to peer and often, without any link to pay.

From Management 3.0 (Jurgen Appelo).

See: Motivation & Recognition

L

Lead time

How long an item takes from being requested to being delivered. Includes waiting before work starts. Compare cycle time.

In DORA's metrics, lead time for changes means commit to production.

See: Delivery Metrics & Tech Debt

Learning zone

The state where a team has both high psychological safety and high standards, so people speak up and stretch. Without standards it's a comfort zone; without safety, an anxiety zone.

From Amy Edmondson.

See: Leading for Psychological Safety · Psychological Safety Check

Levels of conflict

Five stages a conflict can escalate through: problem to solve, disagreement, contest, crusade and world war. The language people use is the best clue to the level, and each level calls for a different response.

From Speed B. Leas; brought to agile teams by Lyssa Adkins in Coaching Agile Teams.

See: Levels of Conflict

M

Minimum viable product (MVP)

The smallest version of a product or feature that tests whether an idea is worth pursuing. It's for learning, not a cut-down release.

See: Epic Breakdown Canvas · Project Kickoff

MoSCoW

Sorting scope into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have (this time), usually for a fixed date or release.

From Dai Clegg; part of the DSDM method.

See: Prioritisation Board

Moving Motivators

An exercise where each person ranks ten intrinsic motivators (such as curiosity, mastery and freedom) by importance, then moves each up or down to show how a change would affect it.

From Management 3.0 (Jurgen Appelo).

See: Motivation & Recognition

N

Now / Next / Later roadmap

A roadmap that groups work by certainty instead of dates: Now is committed and in progress, Next is shaped and ready, and Later holds problems worth exploring. Each item links to a goal.

Popularised by Janna Bastow (ProdPad).

See: Now · Next · Later Roadmap

O

OKR

Objectives and Key Results: a few inspiring objectives, each with measurable key results that show whether it was achieved.

Developed at Intel by Andy Grove; spread by John Doerr.

See: Objectives & Key Results

Onboarding

Helping a new team member get set up, understand how the team works and contribute, usually with a plan, a buddy and check-ins over the first few months.

See: Team Onboarding

Outcome

A change in customer or business behaviour or results, such as more repeat orders. Compare output, the things you build.

See: Value Metrics · Outcome Status Report

P

Pair programming

Two people working on one task at one screen, swapping roles regularly. Mob or ensemble programming is the whole group working together.

From Extreme Programming (XP).

See: Pairing & Mobbing

Persona

A short, evidence-based description of a type of user: their context, goals and frustrations.

See: Persona & Journey Map · Product Vision Board

Personal user manual

A short guide each person writes to working with them: when they work best, how to reach them, how they like feedback and what people misread about them.

Popularised by Atlassian's Team Playbook.

See: Personal User Manual

Planning period

A fixed span, often a quarter, for which several teams plan together.

SAFe calls this a Program Increment (PI).

See: Cross-Team Planning

Planning poker

An estimation technique: everyone reveals a card at once (often 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13), the highest and lowest explain, and the group re-votes. It avoids anchoring on the first opinion.

Described by James Grenning; popularised by Mike Cohn.

See: Sprint Planning · Story Splitting

Prime Directive

A statement read at the start of a retrospective: everyone did the best job they could with what they knew at the time. It sets a tone of learning, not blame.

From Norm Kerth's book Project Retrospectives.

See: Retrospective Kit · Blameless Incident Review

Product goal

The longer-term objective a product is working toward. Backlog items should move toward it.

A Scrum term.

See: Product Vision Board

Psychological safety

A shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks: to speak up, ask for help, admit a mistake or disagree. It isn't about being nice or lowering standards: the goal is high safety and high standards.

From Amy Edmondson's research.

See: Psychological Safety Check · Leading for Psychological Safety

R

RACI

A matrix showing who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for each piece of work. Exactly one person is accountable.

See: RACI & Decision Log

Refinement

The ongoing work of clarifying, splitting and ordering backlog items so the top of the backlog is ready.

Scrum calls it Product Backlog refinement; it isn't a formal event.

See: Story Splitting · Example Mapping

Release Train Engineer (RTE)

The facilitator and servant leader for a team of teams: runs big-room planning, the regular cross-team sync, system demos and inspect & adapt, and clears impediments no single team can.

SAFe's name for the role. Scrum@Scale's Chief Scrum Master is similar.

See: Cross-Team Planning · Cross-Team Sync · Cross-Team Retro

Replenishment

A regular meeting where a team chooses which options to pull into its system next, to fill free WIP capacity.

A Kanban Method cadence.

See: Kanban Cadences · Events & Cadences

Retrospective

A regular session where the team inspects how it works and agrees improvements to try.

See: Retrospective Kit · Cross-Team Retro

RICE

A scoring model: Reach × Impact × Confidence, divided by Effort. Suits product teams that have usage data.

Developed at Intercom.

See: Prioritisation Board

ROAM

A way to close out each risk with a decision: Resolved, Owned, Accepted or Mitigated.

From SAFe's PI Planning.

See: RAID Log · Cross-Team Planning

Rollback

Undoing a release by returning to the previous version. The alternative, fixing the problem with a new release, is called rolling forward or fixing forward.

See: Release Readiness & Strategy · Blameless Incident Review

S

Safe-to-fail experiment

A small, reversible probe in a complex situation, designed so that failure is cheap and teaches something; run several in parallel and amplify the ones that work.

From the Cynefin® framework (Dave Snowden).

See: Cynefin® Sense-Making · 1% Experiment Canvas

SBI (Situation–Behaviour–Impact)

A way to give specific feedback: describe the situation, the observable behaviour, and its impact, then listen. Works for praise as well as correction.

From the Center for Creative Leadership.

See: Feedback Conversation

SDLC (software development lifecycle)

The stages software goes through from idea to use: typically discover, design, build, test, release, operate and learn. Traditional SDLCs run them once, in order; agile delivery runs the loop many times.

See: SDLC at a Glance

Service delivery review

A regular review of flow and service levels, such as lead time and SLE, against what customers expect.

A Kanban Method cadence.

See: Kanban Cadences

Service level expectation (SLE)

A forecast of how long a single item is likely to take, such as “85% of items finish within 8 days”.

From the Kanban Guide.

See: Ageing WIP & SLE

Shift left

Finding problems earlier in the lifecycle, when they're cheaper to fix: agreeing examples before coding, and checking quality, security and accessibility as you go.

See: Test Strategy · Example Mapping

Spike

Time-boxed research to answer a question or reduce uncertainty. Its output is knowledge, not product.

From Extreme Programming (XP).

See: Story Splitting · Backlog Item Anatomy

Sprint goal

The single objective for a sprint, explaining why it's valuable. It guides trade-offs when plans change.

A Scrum term.

See: Sprint Planning

Sprint Zero

A short, timeboxed set-up period before a team's first iteration: forming the team, agreeing the vision, building a first backlog and setting up tools. Also called Iteration 0 or a team launch.

Not part of the Scrum Guide, which expects every sprint to deliver something usable. Disciplined Agile calls it inception.

See: Team Launch (Sprint Zero) · Project Kickoff

Stakeholder

Anyone affected by, or able to affect, the work: users, customers, sponsors, other teams, regulators.

See: Stakeholder Map

Stand-up

A short daily team meeting, often held standing to keep it brief. See Daily Scrum.

See: Daily Sync

Story

A thin, vertical slice of a feature that a user can use, small enough to finish in a few days. Often written “As a…, I want…, so that…”.

From Extreme Programming (XP). Scrum uses the neutral term Product Backlog Item.

See: Backlog Item Anatomy · Story Splitting · User Story Map

Story map

A layout of the user's journey as activities and tasks, with stories underneath, sliced into releases.

From Jeff Patton.

See: User Story Map

Story points

A relative measure of the size of backlog items, combining effort, complexity and uncertainty. Used with velocity for forecasting. Many teams now count items instead.

Common in Scrum teams, but not part of the Scrum Guide.

See: Sprint Planning · Release Forecast & Burn-up

SVG (editable image)

The file format of the boards: an image that stays sharp at any size and holds real, editable text. Open it in a web browser, add it to Miro, Mural or FigJam as a background, or change the words and colours in Figma or Inkscape. On the site it's labelled “Editable image”.

See:

Swarming

When several team members stop starting new work and gather on one item to finish it, often because it's blocked or ageing.

See: Buffering & Swarming · Pairing & Mobbing

System demo

A demo of the integrated work of several teams, so stakeholders see the whole product working.

A SAFe event.

See: Events & Cadences · Sprint Review

T

T-shirt sizing

Rough relative sizing using XS, S, M, L and XL, useful for many items or for roadmaps.

See: Estimation

Task

A step the team takes to finish a story. Not tracked as value on its own.

See: Backlog Item Anatomy

Technical debt

The future cost of shortcuts or outdated design in a system. Its “interest” is the extra effort it adds to every change.

The metaphor is from Ward Cunningham.

See: Delivery Metrics & Tech Debt

Test pyramid

A guide to balancing automated tests: many fast unit tests, fewer integration tests and a small number of slow end-to-end tests.

Popularised by Mike Cohn.

See: Test Strategy

Throughput

The number of items a team finishes in a period. Used for forecasting.

See: Release Forecast & Burn-up · Kanban Board Design

Timebox

A fixed maximum time for an activity. When it's up, you stop and decide what's next.

See: Lean Coffee · Example Mapping

Trunk-based development

Working in small changes merged to one main branch (the trunk) at least daily, instead of long-lived branches that are hard to merge.

See: Continuous Delivery Health Check · Delivery Pipeline Canvas

V

Value stream

All the steps needed to deliver value to a customer, from request to use. Teams are often organised around them.

See: Team Topology Design · Value Metrics

Value stream mapping

Mapping every step from request to customer, with work and wait times, to find delays and improve flow.

A lean technique, popularised by Mike Rother and John Shook.

See: Value Stream Map

Velocity

The amount of work, often in story points, a team completes per iteration. Useful for the team's own forecasting; never for comparing teams.

See: Release Forecast & Burn-up · Sprint Planning

Vertical slice

A piece of work that cuts through every layer (interface, logic, data) so a user can use it. The opposite of splitting by technical layer.

See: Story Splitting · Backlog Item Anatomy

W

WIP (work in progress)

The number of items started but not finished. Limiting WIP shortens cycle time and improves focus.

See: Kanban Board Design · Portfolio Kanban & Investment Mix

Work item age

How long an item that isn't finished yet has been in progress. A leading signal of risk.

From the Kanban Guide.

See: Ageing WIP & SLE

Working agreement

A short set of norms a team agrees on, such as how it communicates, decides and handles interruptions.

See: Team Charter · AI Working Agreement

WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First)

Ranking work by cost of delay divided by job size, so the most value per unit of effort, soonest, goes first.

SAFe's name for CD3, from Don Reinertsen's work on cost of delay.

See: Prioritisation Board · Portfolio Kanban & Investment Mix
