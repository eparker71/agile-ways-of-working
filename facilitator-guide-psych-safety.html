“What would make it easier to say what you really think here?”

Psychological safety is the shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks: to speak up, ask for help, admit a mistake or disagree. Amy Edmondson's research, and Google's Project Aristotle, found it to be one of the strongest signs of an effective team. This board uses seven statements, written for this toolkit and inspired by Edmondson's work, with an anonymous five-point vote. It's a conversation starter, not a validated survey. What matters most is how the team and its leaders respond to the results.

1Before the session

  • Print the board at A3 or 11×17, or run the vote in an anonymous online form
  • Agree with leaders that they'll step out while the team votes, and won't ask who voted what
  • Decide in advance who sees the results; by default, only the team
  • Bring dot stickers or markers
  • Think about facilitation: someone outside the team's reporting line often helps

2Agenda & run-sheet

TimeSectionFacilitator notes
0–5WhyExplain the purpose and the rules: anonymous, no one explains their vote, results stay with the team.
5–151 · The CheckEveryone places one dot per statement, privately. Turn the board away, or use an online form.
15–202 · ResultsCount agree and disagree for each statement. Note the change from last time.
20–353 · What We NoticeDiscuss patterns: which statements are lowest, and what might explain it? Talk about situations, not people.
35–454 · What Leaders Can DoIf leaders rejoin, share the patterns and ask them to pick behaviours to practise.
45–555 · Our ExperimentsAgree one or two small experiments and when to check them.

3Reading the results

Look for patterns, not a single score.

Mistakes & risk (1, 4)

  • Low here: people hide errors
  • Celebrate learning from failure

Speaking up (2, 7)

  • Low here: quiet meetings
  • Ask for dissent explicitly

Belonging (3, 6)

  • Low here: people feel outside
  • Check who's not being heard

Help (5)

  • Low here: people struggle alone
  • Normalise asking

Change over time

  • Trend matters most
  • Re-run every 2–3 months

4Facilitation tips

Anonymity is everythingIf people suspect they can be identified, the results will look better than reality.
Leaders model itSafety grows when leaders admit mistakes, ask questions and thank people for bad news.
Talk about situations“When we discussed X, it felt risky to disagree” helps. Naming people doesn't.
Small experimentsTry one change, such as round-robin input or a devil's advocate role, and check it.
Don't shoot the messengerA low score is a gift of honesty. Thank the team for it.
Not a performance measureNever use the results to evaluate a manager or team.
Safety isn't comfortHigh safety with low standards is a comfort zone. Aim for the learning zone: safe to speak up, and high standards.
Common pitfalls: leaders in the room during voting; asking who voted low; sharing results without the team's agreement; treating it as a score to hit; no follow-up; running it once and never again.

5After the session

Straight away

Photograph the results, then clear the board so votes aren't on display.

Next few weeks

Run the experiments and notice what changes in meetings.

Each retro

Check in: is it easier to speak up? What's helping?

Every 2–3 months

Re-run the check and compare. Feed themes into the Team Health Check.

6How it fits the toolkit

Psychological safety underpins everything else. Use it alongside the Team Health Check, run safer retros from the Retro Kit, agree norms in the Team Charter, and use the Coaching Conversation canvas for one-to-one follow-up. Leaders can use the Leading for Psychological Safety poster for daily habits.

Create an editable whiteboard for “Psychological Safety Check”, a workshop board.

Purpose: An anonymous check of how safe it feels to speak up, take risks and admit mistakes, and what to do about it.

Layout: a wide landscape board (about 16:11). Make one frame per section below, placed as described. In each frame, add the section name as a heading, then its content as short text labels or empty fields to fill in. Leave space in each frame for sticky notes. Use the header colour given. Keep the wording as written and don't add extra content.
Key: Dot = One anonymous vote · Yellow Sticky = Observation

Sections:
1. The Check: top left, about 65% wide and 55% tall; header colour purple.
   Subtitle: One dot per statement · vote privately
   Content: Strongly disagree: · Disagree: · Neutral: · Agree: · Strongly agree: · 1. I can admit a mistake here without it being held against me. · 2. We raise problems and difficult topics openly. · 3. Different views and backgrounds are welcomed on this team. · 4. It's safe to try something new here, even if it might fail. · 5. I can ask anyone on the team for help. · 6. People on this team have my back. · 7. My ideas are listened to and used.
2. Run It Safely: top right, about 35% wide and 55% tall; header colour slate grey.
   Subtitle: Safety first, or the results lie
   Content: Votes are anonymous (board turned away, or an online form) · Leaders and managers step out while voting · Results stay with the team unless it chooses to share · No one is asked to explain their vote · Talk about patterns, not individuals · Results · Statement: · Mostly agree: · Mostly disagree: · Change: · Re-run every 2–3 months to see the trend.
3. What We Notice: bottom left, about 35% wide and 40% tall; header colour blue.
   Subtitle: Patterns, not people
   Content: Where are votes lowest? What might explain it? · What would make it easier to speak up?
4. What Leaders Can Do: bottom centre, about 35% wide and 40% tall; header colour emerald green.
   Subtitle: Model it
   Content: Frame work as learning, not just doing · Admit your own mistakes and doubts · Ask genuine questions, then listen · Thank people for bad news and dissent · Respond to failure with curiosity, not blame · Make it clear every voice is wanted · Follow up on what people raise
5. Our Experiments: bottom right, about 35% wide and 40% tall; header colour dark green.
   Subtitle: Small changes · check them
   Content: Experiment: · Who: · Check on:

Footer questions: “What would make it easier to say what you really think here?” “When did someone last speak up, and what happened next?”
