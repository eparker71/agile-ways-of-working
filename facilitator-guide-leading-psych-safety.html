Leading for Psychological Safety Guide

How leaders can build a team where people speak up, ask for help and admit mistakes, without lowering standards.

“When did someone last tell you something you didn't want to hear, and how did you react?”

Psychological safety, the shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks, is built or broken mostly by how leaders behave. Amy Edmondson describes three moves: set the stage by framing work as uncertain and everyone's input as needed; invite participation through genuine questions and structures for input; and respond productively to bad news, questions and failure. The poster turns these into things to do and say, plus daily habits and warning signs. Its safety and standards grid answers a common worry: psychological safety isn't about being nice or lowering the bar. The goal is the learning zone, where both are high. 3The three moves

What each move looks like in practice.

Set the Stage

  • Frame work as learning
  • Admit uncertainty
  • Explain why input matters

Invite Participation

  • Genuine questions
  • Structures for input
  • Speak last

Respond Productively

  • Thank the messenger
  • Curiosity about failure
  • Fair consequences for misconduct

Safety and Standards

  • Aim for the learning zone
  • Safety without standards is comfort

Warning Signs

  • Silence when you speak
  • Late bad news
  • Always-green status

4Facilitation tips

Go firstAdmitting your own mistakes and doubts gives everyone else permission to do the same.
Your reaction is the messageOne dismissive response to bad news can undo months of encouragement.
Ask, then waitSilence after a question feels long. Waiting is what lets people answer.
Keep the bar highSafety and accountability work together. Be clear about expectations and safe about problems.
Practise the phrasesSaying them aloud in the session makes them easier to use under pressure.
Ask for feedbackAsk the team, or a coach, how safe it feels to challenge you, and act on what you hear.
Common pitfalls: treating safety as being nice or avoiding conflict; lowering standards; announcing safety instead of showing it; reacting badly to the first piece of bad news; tracking it as a team score; doing it once.

5After the session

This week

Try your chosen habits and phrases in real meetings.

Each week

Notice the warning signs, and one moment you handled well or badly.

In 4–6 weeks

Check in with a coach or peer. Re-run the team's Psychological Safety Check.

Ongoing

Keep the poster visible, and add habits as earlier ones become natural.

Be open that you don't have all the answers. · Do: · ✓ (×3) · Explain why each person's view matters · Name the risks and unknowns out loud · Share a mistake of your own · Say: · “This is new for all of us.” · “I could be wrong about this.”

   Subtitle: Ask, then listen
   Content: Ask genuine questions and build ways for people to contribute, especially the quiet ones. · Do: · ✓ (×3) · Ask open questions, then wait · Use silent writing and round-robins · Speak last in discussions · Say: · “What are we missing?” · “Who sees this differently?”
3. Respond Productively: top right, about 35% wide and 35% tall.
   Subtitle: What you do next decides it
   Content: How you react to bad news, questions and failure tells people whether to speak again. · Do: · ✓ (×3) · Thank people for raising problems · Treat failure as learning: ask what happened · Still address real misconduct, fairly · Say: · “Thank you for telling me early.” · “What can we learn from this?”

   Subtitle: Safety is not the same as comfort

   Content: Try saying: · Avoid saying: · “What would you do?” · “Who's responsible for this?” · “Help me understand.” · “That's not how we do it here.” · “What did we learn?” · “Why didn't you tell me sooner?” · “I'd value your view on this.” · “Any questions?
