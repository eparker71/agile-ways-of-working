“Where does work wait, and what are people doing while it waits?”

A waste walk is a lean habit: instead of discussing a process in a meeting room, you go where the work happens (the gemba) and watch it, looking for the eight wastes: hand-offs, partly done work, task switching, waiting, features nobody uses, over-processing, defects and unused skills. In knowledge work the “walk” might be following one piece of work through its tools and queues, sitting with the people who do it, or watching a hand-off happen. The board collects what you see under each waste, adds up roughly what each costs per week, and ends with one change to try. The Lean Principles poster explains the eight wastes.

1Before the session

  • Print the board at A3 or 11×17, or share it on screen
  • Pick one process with a clear start and end, such as “request to release” or “new joiner to first commit”
  • Ask the people who do the work if you can watch, and explain it's about the process, not them
  • Bring pink stickies and pens for every walker

2Agenda & run-sheet

TimeSectionFacilitator notes
0–10Set up the walkAgree the process, start and end. Read the ground rules aloud.
10–70Go and seeFollow real work. Ask “what happens next?” and “what are you waiting for?” One sticky per waste.
70–85SortPut each sticky under its waste. Tally how often each was seen.
85–95Cost itRoughly how many hours a week does each waste cost? Rank by cost, not count.
95–110Remove onePick the costliest waste you can change. Agree a countermeasure, an owner and a check date.

3What to look for

In knowledge work, most waste hides between people and tools, not inside tasks.

Waiting

  • Approvals, answers, environments
  • Usually the biggest

Partly done work

  • Started, not finished
  • Hidden in long queues

Hand-offs

  • Each one loses knowledge
  • and adds a wait

Task switching

  • Too much on the go
  • No time to finish

Rework

  • Defects and misunderstandings
  • Found late, fixed twice

4Facilitation tips

Watch, don't auditPeople will show you the real process only if they trust it's not about them.
Follow the workPick one item and trace it end to end; it shows waits that no one person sees.
Ask about waiting“What are you waiting for?” finds more waste than “What are you doing?”
Facts, not namesWrite what happened, never who did it.
Cost beats countOne long wait can cost more than twenty small hand-offs. Rank by hours lost.
Remove one thingA single change that sticks beats a list of twenty that don't.
Common pitfalls: walking the official process instead of the real one; blaming individuals; collecting wastes and doing nothing; trying to remove everything at once; walking other teams' work but not your own.

5After the session

This week

Start the countermeasure, and make the waste visible on the team board.

At the check date

Did the waste shrink? Keep the change, adjust it, or try another.

Next quarter

Walk the same process again and compare.

Go deeper

Map the whole flow with the Value Stream Map, or take a stubborn waste through an A3.

Read the eight wastes on the Lean Principles poster first. Map the whole flow and its waits with the Value Stream Map, take a stubborn waste through A3 Problem-Solving, and track blocked time with Blocker Clustering.
