Lean Principles Guide

How to use the Lean Principles poster to introduce lean thinking, spot waste in your own work, and pick one thing to improve.

“Which of the eight wastes costs us most, and where do we see it?”

Lean grew out of the Toyota Production System, developed by Taiichi Ohno and others. The name was coined by John Krafcik and popularised in The Machine That Changed the World (1990); James Womack and Daniel Jones then set out its five principles in Lean Thinking (1996). Its core idea is simple: deliver what the customer values, and steadily remove everything else. Much of this toolkit comes from lean: Kanban, the Value Stream Map, WIP limits, root cause analysis and small, continuous improvement. The poster sets out the five principles (value, value stream, flow, pull and perfection), the eight wastes in a factory and in knowledge work, the two pillars of continuous improvement and respect for people, and four habits to try. The knowledge-work wastes are adapted from Mary and Tom Poppendieck's Lean Software Development.

1Before the session

  • Print the poster at A3 or 11×17, or share it on screen
  • Bring a recent piece of work that took longer than expected, with its rough timeline
  • Have stickies in two colours: one for wastes you see, one for ideas
  • If you can, look at the team's board first: what's waiting, blocked or half done?

2Agenda & run-sheet

TimeSectionFacilitator notes
0–10What is value?Read principle 1. Who is our customer, and what would they pay for, or miss?
10–20The five principlesWalk across the poster. Where are we strong, and where weak?
20–35Waste spottingRead the knowledge-work column. Everyone writes the wastes they see in our work, one per sticky.
35–45Biggest wasteCluster the stickies under the eight wastes and dot-vote the one that costs most.
45–55One habitPick a habit from the poster, or a tool it points to, to tackle that waste.
55–60Next stepAgree who does what by when, and when you'll look again.

3The five principles

Each principle comes with a question, and points to tools in this kit.

Value

  • Defined by the customer
  • Who is it for, what will change?

Value stream

  • Every step, request to customer
  • Question steps that add nothing

Flow

  • Small batches, no waiting
  • Where does work wait?

Pull

  • Start when there's capacity
  • Stop starting, start finishing

Perfection

  • Small, endless improvement
  • One thing this week

4Facilitation tips

Value comes firstRemoving waste only helps if you know what the customer values. Start there.
Waste isn't lazinessMost waste is built into the system: hand-offs, approvals, queues. Fix the system, not the person.
Waiting is the big oneIn knowledge work, items usually spend far longer waiting than being worked on.
Look before you fixGo and see the work, or map it, before deciding what's wrong.
Small stepsOne change at a time, measured, beats a big lean programme.
Lean isn't cutting peopleRespect for people is a pillar: lean frees people's time for valuable work.
Common pitfalls: treating lean as cost-cutting or headcount reduction; removing steps without asking what they protect; spotting waste in other teams but not your own; a one-off waste hunt with no follow-up; confusing being busy with flow.

5After the session

Straight away

Start the chosen habit or tool, and make the waste visible on the team board.

In two weeks

Check at the retro: did the waste shrink? Keep, change or drop the change.

Next month

Map the whole flow with the Value Stream Map to find the biggest waits.

Ongoing

Use the Marginal Gains Workshop and 1% Experiment Canvas to keep improving in small steps.

6How it fits the toolkit

The poster points to the toolkit's lean tools: the Value Stream Map for the whole flow, Kanban Board Design and Ageing WIP for flow and WIP limits, Root Cause Analysis to stop and fix, and the Marginal Gains Workshop and 1% Experiment Canvas for continuous improvement.

Create an editable whiteboard for “Lean Principles”, a workshop board.

Purpose: The ideas behind flow, Kanban and continuous improvement: deliver value, remove waste, and keep getting a little better.

Layout: a wide landscape board (about 16:11). Make one frame per section below, placed as described. In each frame, add the section name as a heading, then its content as short text labels or empty fields to fill in. Leave space in each frame for sticky notes. Use the header colour given. Keep the wording as written and don't add extra content.

Header fields to fill in: Team, Date

Sections:
1. Value: top left, about 20% wide and 40% tall.
   Content: Define value from the customer's point of view: · what they would pay for, or miss if it were gone. · Ask: · “Who is this for, and what will change for them?” · Tools in this kit: · Product Vision Board · Persona & Journey Map
2. Value stream: top left, about 20% wide and 40% tall; header colour purple.
   Content: Map every step from request to customer, and question each step that adds no value. · Ask: · “Which steps would the customer pay for?” · Tools in this kit: · Value Stream Map · Waste Walk
3. Flow: top centre, about 20% wide and 40% tall; header colour sky blue.
   Content: Make work move smoothly in small batches, without waiting, hand-offs or interruptions. · Ask: · “Where does work wait, and why?” · Tools in this kit: · Kanban Board Design · Ageing WIP & SLE
4. Pull: top right, about 20% wide and 40% tall.
   Content: Start new work only when there is capacity and a real need, not because a plan said so. · Ask: · “Are we starting more than we finish?” · Tools in this kit: · Kanban Cadences · Buffering & Swarming
5. Perfection: top right, about 20% wide and 40% tall; header colour amber.
   Content: Keep improving in small steps. There is always more waste to remove, so the work never ends. · Ask: · “What one small thing could we improve this week?” · Tools in this kit: · Marginal Gains Workshop · 1% Experiment Canvas
6. The Eight Wastes: bottom left, about 65% wide and 55% tall; header colour red.
   Subtitle: Anything that doesn't add value for the customer
   Content: Waste: · In a factory: · In knowledge work: · Transport · Moving parts between places · Hand-offs between people and teams · Inventory · Stock waiting to be used · Partly done work: started, not finished · Motion · People walking and searching · Switching between tasks and tools · Waiting · Idle until the last step finishes · Waiting for approvals, answers, environments · Overproduction · Making more than is ordered · Features nobody asked for or uses · Over-processing · More work than the customer needs · Gold-plating, extra reports, re-doing work · Defects · Faulty parts and rework · Bugs, misunderstood requirements, rework · Unused skills · Ignoring workers' ideas · Not using what people know, or can learn · Taiichi Ohno named the first seven; unused skills was added later. Knowledge-work versions adapted from Mary and Tom Poppendieck, Lean Software Development.
7. Two Pillars: middle right, about 35% wide and 25% tall; header colour slate grey.
   Subtitle: Both, not either
   Content: Continuous improvement · Small, frequent changes by the people who do the work (kaizen). · Respect for people · Trust people to solve problems, and fix the system, not the person.
8. Lean Habits: bottom right, about 35% wide and 30% tall; header colour dark green.
   Subtitle: Try one this week
   Content: Go and see · Watch the work where it happens (gemba). · Stop and fix · When something goes wrong, fix the cause. · Make it visible · Put the work and its problems on a board. · Small batches · Finish small pieces often, not big ones late.

Footer questions: “Which of the eight wastes costs us most, and where do we see it?” “What would the customer happily pay for, and what wouldn't they?”
