Backlog Item Anatomy Guide

How to use the poster to help teams write good epics, features and stories, and spot weak ones.

“Who is this for, and what will be better for them?”

Teams often write epics that are just big tasks, features that are technical layers, and stories that deliver nothing on their own. The poster shows each level with a definition, a template, a good example and a weak example, using one running example so people can see how a big bet becomes a thin slice. It also covers INVEST (from Bill Wake), acceptance criteria in Given / When / Then form, and the other kinds of backlog item. Names vary by framework and tool: in SAFe an epic sits above features, while in Jira an epic sits directly above stories. Use the levels your organisation uses, and see the glossary for the differences.

1Before the session

  • Print the poster at A3 and pin it where the team refines its backlog
  • Pick 6–10 real backlog items: a mix of epics, features and stories, good and bad
  • Write each on a card or sticky, without saying which level it is
  • Bring the team's Definition of Ready, if it has one

2Agenda & run-sheet

TimeStepFacilitator notes
0–10Walk the posterRead the running example from top to bottom. Ask: how does the story serve the epic's outcome?
10–25SortIn pairs, sort the real items: which level is each, and is it good or weak? Use the poster's templates.
25–45RewriteRewrite the weak ones using the templates. Split anything that's too big with the Story Splitting poster.
45–55Agree standardsAgree two or three standards the team will apply, and add them to the Definition of Ready.
55–60ClosePick who will tidy the rest of the backlog, and when.

3What good looks like at each level

Quick checks to apply when writing or refining items.

Epic

  • An outcome, not a solution
  • A measure that could prove it wrong
  • Broken into features

Feature

  • Usable on its own
  • A clear benefit to a named user
  • Fits in a planning period

Story

  • A vertical slice
  • Passes INVEST
  • Has acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria

  • Given · When · Then
  • Concrete and testable
  • Agreed before work starts

Task · Spike · Bug · Enabler

  • Tasks live inside stories
  • Spikes are time-boxed
  • Enablers make future value possible

4Facilitation tips

Start from the userEvery level should say who benefits. If it can't, it's probably a task.
Outcomes at the topEpics need a measure. Without one, nobody can tell when to stop investing.
Watch for “and”A story with “and” in it is usually two stories.
Avoid developer stories“As a developer, I want…” almost always hides a technical task. Find the user story it serves.
Criteria make it testableWriting two or three Given / When / Then examples often reveals the story is too big.
Templates are a startA good conversation matters more than perfect wording. The template prompts the conversation.
Common pitfalls: using the story template for everything, including tasks; epics with no outcome or measure; features split by technical layer; acceptance criteria written after the work; arguing about labels instead of value.

5After the session

Straight away

Leave the poster up in the team space and refer to it in refinement.

Next refinement

Check new items against the poster before they're ready.

Over time

Add your own good examples from the team's real backlog.

With stakeholders

Share the poster when stakeholders bring requests, so ideas arrive as outcomes.

6How it fits the toolkit

The break-down tools work from big to small: the Epic Breakdown Canvas turns an idea into an MVP and features, the Story Map lays out the user journey and release slices, the Story Splitting poster makes stories small enough for a sprint, and Example Mapping checks that each story is understood and ready.
