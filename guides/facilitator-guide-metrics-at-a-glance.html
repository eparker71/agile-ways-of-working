Delivery Metrics at a Glance Guide

How to use the poster to agree what each delivery metric means, pick the few that answer your questions, and avoid the usual traps.

“Which metric would tell us most about our biggest problem right now?”

Teams and leaders often use the same words for different things. Lead time to one person is cycle time to another, and velocity gets compared across teams it was never meant to compare. The poster sets out ten common delivery metrics: for each, what it measures, the question it answers, how it gets misused, and the tool in this kit that uses it. It adds Little's Law, which links work in progress, throughput and cycle time and explains why limiting work in progress speeds things up; a guide to which metric answers which question; and five rules for using metrics well. The flow metrics draw on Daniel Vacanti's Actionable Agile Metrics for Predictability, the four delivery metrics on the DORA research reported in Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble and Kim), and Little's Law on John Little's 1961 proof.

4Facilitation tips

Agree where the clock startsMost lead time and cycle time arguments are really about start and end points. Write yours down.
Percentiles beat averages“85% of items finish within 9 days” is more useful, and more honest, than an average.
Count before you estimateMany teams forecast as well by counting items as with story points, with less effort.
Watch the ageWork item age shows trouble while you can still act; cycle time only shows it afterwards.
Few, and for the teamThree to five metrics the team uses beat twenty in a report nobody reads.
Never per personMetrics are for the system of work. Measuring people changes their behaviour, not the system.
Common pitfalls: comparing velocity between teams; setting a metric as a target; reporting averages without the spread; speed measures with no quality measure beside them; tracking metrics nobody acts on; changing definitions between reports.

5After the session

Straight away

Write down your agreed definitions, and where each metric comes from.

Each iteration

Look at your few metrics as a team, as trends, and ask what they're telling you.

Each quarter

Drop a metric nobody used, and check none has become a target.

Ongoing

Use the Ageing WIP, Release Forecast and Delivery Metrics boards to act on what you see.

Create an editable whiteboard for “Delivery Metrics at a Glance”, a workshop board.

Purpose: What each delivery metric measures, the question it answers, and how it gets misused.

Layout: a wide landscape board (about 16:11). Make one frame per section below, placed as described. In each frame, add the section name as a heading, then its content as short text labels or empty fields to fill in. Leave space in each frame for sticky notes. Use the header colour given. Keep the wording as written and don't add extra content.

Header fields to fill in: Team, Date

Sections:
1. Velocity: top left, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Story points a team finishes in an iteration. · Asks: · “How much can we plan into the next iteration?” · Misused when: · Set as a target, or compared across teams. · IN THIS KIT: Estimation
2. Throughput: top left, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Items finished per week or iteration, counted. · Asks: · “How many items can we expect next month?” · Misused when: · Counting items of very different sizes. · IN THIS KIT: Release Forecast & Burn-up
3. Cycle time: top centre, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Time from starting an item to finishing it. · Asks: · “Once we start, how long does it take?” · Misused when: · Averages hide the slow ones: use percentiles. · IN THIS KIT: Ageing WIP & SLE
4. Lead time: top right, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Time from request to delivery to the customer. · Asks: · “How long does the customer wait?” · Misused when: · Not saying where the clock starts. · IN THIS KIT: Value Stream Map
5. Work item age: top right, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · How long an unfinished item has been in progress. · Asks: · “Which items are stuck right now?” · Misused when: · Only looking once items finish. · IN THIS KIT: Ageing WIP & SLE
6. Work in progress: middle left, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Items started but not yet finished. · Asks: · “Are we starting more than we finish?” · Misused when: · Raising the limit whenever it's hit. · IN THIS KIT: Kanban Board Design
7. Flow efficiency: middle left, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Share of lead time spent working, not waiting. · Asks: · “How much of the time is waiting?” · Misused when: · Making people busier, not cutting waits. · IN THIS KIT: Value Stream Map
8. Cumulative flow: middle centre, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Items in each state over time, as bands. · Asks: · “Where is work piling up?” · Misused when: · A widening band is a queue: act on it. · IN THIS KIT: Kanban Cadences
9. Burn-up and burndown: middle right, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Work done, and total scope, over time. · Asks: · “Will we finish by the date?” · Misused when: · A burndown hides added scope. · IN THIS KIT: Release Forecast & Burn-up
10. DORA four: middle right, about 20% wide and 30% tall.
   Content: Measures: · Deploy frequency, lead time for changes, failure rate, time to restore. · Asks: · “Are we delivering faster and more safely?” · Misused when: · Chasing speed and ignoring stability. · IN THIS KIT: Delivery Metrics & Tech Debt
11. Little's Law: bottom left, about 35% wide and 40% tall; header colour sky blue.
   Subtitle: Why limiting WIP speeds things up
   Content: Cycle time = WIP / Throughput · (average items in progress / average items finished per week) · 20 items in progress / 5 finished a week = about 4 weeks for each item. · Halve the work in progress to 10, at the same throughput, and items take about 2 weeks. · Holds for averages over a stable period. From queueing theory (John Little, 1961).
12. Which Metric for Which Question?: bottom centre, about 35% wide and 40% tall.
   Subtitle: Start with the question
   Content: When will it be done?: throughput, as a forecast range · What's stuck right now?: work item age · Why is it slow?: flow efficiency, cumulative flow · What can we promise?: 85th percentile cycle time · How much fits next?: velocity or throughput · Are releases safe?: change failure rate, time to restore
13. Use Metrics Well: bottom right, about 35% wide and 40% tall.
   Subtitle: Goodhart's law applies
   Content: Teams, never individuals · Personal metrics get gamed and hurt teamwork. · Ranges, not single numbers · “13–20 June” is honest; “15 June” isn't. · Trends, not snapshots · One iteration tells you little; six tell a story. · Speed with quality · Pair every speed measure with a quality one. · Questions, not targets · When a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring.

Footer questions: “Which metric would tell us most about our biggest problem right now?” “Which of our measures has quietly become a target?”
