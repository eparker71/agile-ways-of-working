“Where does a change wait longest on its way to customers?”

DevOps started around 2009 as a movement to close the gap between the people who build software and the people who run it; Patrick Debois named the first DevOpsDays conference. It isn't a job title or a tool: it's a set of ideas about building, releasing and running software as one team. The poster sets out the three ways described by Gene Kim and colleagues in The Phoenix Project and The DevOps Handbook (Kim, Humble, Debois and Willis): flow from idea to customer, feedback from customer to team, and continual learning. It adds CALMS (culture, automation, lean, measurement, sharing), which Jez Humble set out building on John Willis and Damon Edwards' CAMS, as a quick health check, and the four key metrics from the DORA research reported in Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble and Kim). All are described here in our own words.

1Before the session

  • Print the poster at A3 or 11×17, or share it on screen
  • Invite the people who build, test, release and support the service, not just developers
  • Bring rough numbers if you have them: how often you release, and how long a small change takes
  • Have stickies and a marker for the CALMS scores

3The three ways

Each way comes with a question, a few practices, and tools in this kit.

Flow

  • Idea to customer, fast
  • Small batches, fewer hand-offs

Feedback

  • Customer to team, fast
  • Find problems close to the source

Continual learning

  • Failures are information
  • Time to improve daily work

4Facilitation tips

Culture firstTools help, but the shared ownership is what makes DevOps work. Start with how people work together.
Everyone in the roomIf operations, testing or security aren't there, you'll only see half the flow.
Use real examplesTalk about last week's release, not DevOps in general.
Speed and stability togetherTeams that release more often usually fail less, not more. Small changes are safer.
Measure to learnThe four key metrics are for the team to improve, never to compare teams.
Automate one thingPick the most painful manual step and automate just that.
Common pitfalls: renaming the operations team the DevOps team; buying a tool and calling it a transformation; measuring individuals with the four key metrics; leaving security and testing until the end; automating a broken process before fixing it.

5After the session

Straight away

Start the chosen habit, and put the change on the team board.

In two weeks

Check at the retro: did it help? Keep, change or drop it.

Next month

Map the path to production with the Delivery Pipeline Canvas, and start tracking the four key metrics.

Ongoing

Re-score CALMS each quarter, alongside the Continuous Delivery Health Check.

6How it fits the toolkit

The poster ties together the engineering tools. The delivery engineering tools work as a set. SDLC at a Glance shows where each stage of the lifecycle fits. The Delivery Pipeline Canvas maps how changes reach production, the Test Strategy decides how quality is built in, and Release Readiness & Strategy plans each release. The Continuous Delivery Health Check tracks the practices over time, and Delivery Metrics & Tech Debt measures the results. For the wider flow from idea to customer, use the Value Stream Map. For reliability, the SLOs & Error Budgets board agrees how reliable a service needs to be, and the Blameless Incident Review learns from failures.

Purpose: Build, release and run software as one team: fast flow to customers, fast feedback from them, and a habit of learning.

Layout: a wide landscape board (about 16:11). Make one frame per section below, placed as described. In each frame, add the section name as a heading, then its content as short text labels or empty fields to fill in. Leave space in each frame for sticky notes. Use the header colour given. Keep the wording as written and don't add extra content.

Header fields to fill in: Team, Date

Sections:
1. Flow: top left, about 35% wide and 45% tall.
   Subtitle: Idea → customer
   Content: Speed up the whole path from idea to customer: small batches, visible work, fewer hand-offs, and never passing a known problem downstream. · Ask: · “How long does a small change take to reach a customer?” · In practice: · Make the work visible, end to end · Limit work in progress · Shrink batches and hand-offs · Tools in this kit: · Value Stream Map · Delivery Pipeline Canvas · Kanban Board Design
2. Feedback: top centre, about 35% wide and 45% tall.
   Subtitle: Customer → team
   Content: Find problems fast and close to where they start: automated tests, monitoring, and teams who see how their work behaves in production. · Ask: · “How soon would we know if a release hurt customers?” · In practice: · Test automatically, on every change · Watch what users actually experience · Fix the cause, then add a check · Tools in this kit: · Test Strategy · SLOs & Error Budgets · Continuous Delivery Health Check
3. Continual learning: top right, about 35% wide and 45% tall.
   Subtitle: Experiment · improve
   Content: Treat failures as information, run small experiments, and set time aside to improve daily work and share what you learn. · Ask: · “What did our last incident teach us, and what changed?” · In practice: · Blameless reviews after incidents · Time for improvement, every iteration · Share lessons across teams · Tools in this kit: · Blameless Incident Review · 1% Experiment Canvas · Retrospective Kit
4. CALMS: A Quick Health Check: bottom left, about 65% wide and 55% tall; header colour sky blue.
   Subtitle: Five parts of devops · which is weakest here?
   Content: Part: · What it means: · You'll see: · Shared ownership of building and running the work, with trust and no · Developers and operations solve incidents together. · C · Culture · blame. · Automate the repetitive and risky steps: build, test, deploy and · Any change can reach production through one pipeline. · Automation · A · infrastructure. · Small batches, limited work in progress, and less waste on the way to · Changes are small, and merged at least daily. · L · Lean · production. · Measure flow, quality and outcomes, and use them to learn, not to · The team knows how long changes take and how often they fail. · M · Measurement · judge. · Share tools, knowledge and lessons across teams and roles. · Incident reviews and runbooks are open to everyone. · Sharing · S · CALMS was set out by Jez Humble, building on CAMS from John Willis and Damon Edwards. Score each part 1–5 as a team, then improve the lowest.
5. Four Key Metrics: middle right, about 35% wide and 25% tall; header colour slate grey.
   Subtitle: From the DORA research
   Content: Deployment frequency: How often changes reach production · Lead time for changes: From commit to running in production · Change failure rate: Share of changes that cause a problem · Time to restore: How fast service recovers after a failure · Speed and stability go together. Track them on Delivery Metrics.
6. DevOps Habits: bottom right, about 35% wide and 30% tall; header colour dark green.
   Subtitle: Try one this iteration
   Content: You build it, you run it · The team that builds a service helps support it. · Small, frequent changes · Smaller releases are easier to test and undo. · Automate the toil · Turn a repeated manual step into a script. · Blameless by default · Ask what made the mistake easy, not who made it.

Footer questions: “Where does a change wait longest on its way to customers?” “What would make releasing boring?”
